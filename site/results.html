What Stage 1 does not show. Grounded feet are held fixed in the world by construction (tests check it to a nanometre), and the crawl keeps the body centre inside the triangle of grounded feet on paper. That treats the body centre as the centre of mass, and with two feet down (the trot) the body centre is never inside a support polygon: it ends up to mm off the line between the grounded feet. Balance needs physics.

Stage 2: how heavy can it be? (static bound)

The joint torque needed to hold the body's weight in a pose, from vertical foot loads only, with the Stage 2 design placeholders (50/150/150 mm legs, 212.5 mm stand, 300×150 mm body) and the unverified listed stall torque of the servo.

Heaviest total mass a stance can hold

kg; "half" assumes only half the stall torque is available continuously (an assumption, not a datasheet value)

The knee decides: with the foot under the hip, the hip-pitch joint carries almost no static torque. This leaves out leg and servo mass (twelve listed servos are 0.72 kg alone), gait dynamics, friction and heat, so it is an upper bound, not a mass the robot can walk with.

Stage 2: standing and rising in physics

Standing: peak servo torque against robot mass

fraction of the 1.86 N·m stall torque, whole run; light legs, 5 V; open marker = the robot fell

With the default gain (kp 10) the robot stands up to 4 kg and falls at 4.5 kg; with kp 100 it stands up to 5.5 kg and falls at 6 kg. Both fall below the 7.18 kg static bound, and the answer moves with gains that are placeholders.

Rising from a 140 mm crouch

peak servo torque fraction during the rise, kp 10, 0.66° command steps; open marker = did not stand

Faster rises cost torque; the crouch is the hardest pose (25% more knee torque per newton than standing). 5 kg does not rise at any rise time.

The feet do not share the weight equally

percent of the weight on each foot (an equal share would be 25%)

The knees all bend backward, so the weight moves rearward. The mechanism was not traced.
