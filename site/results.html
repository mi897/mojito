What Stage 1 does not show. Grounded feet are held fixed in the world by construction (tests check it to a nanometre), and the crawl keeps the body centre inside the triangle of grounded feet on paper. That treats the body centre as the centre of mass, and with two feet down (the trot) the body centre is never inside a support polygon: it ends up to mm off the line between the grounded feet. Balance needs physics.

kg; "half" assumes only half the stall torque is available continuously (an assumption, not a datasheet value)

The knee decides: with the foot under the hip, the hip-pitch joint carries almost no static torque. This leaves out leg and servo mass (twelve listed servos are 0.72 kg alone), gait dynamics, friction and heat, so it is an upper bound, not a mass the robot can walk with.

Stage 2: standing and rising in physics

Standing: peak servo torque against robot mass

fraction of the 1.86 N·m stall torque, whole run; light legs, 5 V; open marker = the robot fell

With the default gain (kp 10) the robot stands up to 4 kg and falls at 4.5 kg; with kp 100 it stands up to 5.5 kg and falls at 6 kg. Both fall below the 7.18 kg static bound, and the answer moves with gains that are placeholders.

Rising from a 140 mm crouch

peak servo torque fraction during the rise, kp 10, 0.66° command steps; open marker = did not stand

Faster rises cost torque; the crouch is the hardest pose (25% more knee torque per newton than standing). 5 kg does not rise at any rise time.

The feet do not share the weight equally

percent of the weight on each foot (an equal share would be 25%)

The knees all bend backward, so the weight moves rearward. The mechanism was not traced.

Stage 2: walking in physics

Trot and crawl foot paths from Stage 1 go through the closed-form IK and the servo model into the MuJoCo model; the body is free and must push itself along. Default placeholder masses (1.72 kg), 5 V. The trot uses the tuned settings (kp 50, kd 0.9, 0.16 m stance, 22 mm swing; why); the crawl keeps kp 10, kd 0.2 and a 0.2125 m stance. The trot has a 1.7 s period (why); the crawl is 2 s.

All nine runs stayed upright (tilt at most °) but none reached the commanded speed (the tuned trot comes closest). The 0.30 m/s trot is not counted as walked: its servos are saturated for more than 20% of the walk. Marching in place drifts backward for the crawl; the tuned trot does not.

Why the trot falls short of 0.15 m/s

measured body speed, and the speed the loaded feet's commanded motion would give ("ask")

Soft servos (kp 10) explain the crawl shortfall completely. For the 1.7 s trot with the original settings (30 mm swing, 0.2125 m stance, kp 10) the body makes more than the "ask" (the speed the loaded feet's commanded motion would give), so that explanation, worked out at 0.5 s, no longer accounts for the numbers; the reason was not traced. A taller swing made it worse.

Finding: the 0.5 s trot was not the best period; a sweep gave 1.7 s

The Stage 1 trot has a 0.5 s period. It was chosen for the geometry demos, not tuned in physics, and it turned out to be a poor choice there. I first tried 1 s, which was worse still, so the period was swept. Here only the period changes (duty 0.5, diagonal pairs, 30 mm swing) and the same physics is run for each. One run per cell. 1.7 s was picked from this sweep by a rule written after seeing the runs (the longest period where every forward run up to 0.30 m/s walks with tilt under 10°; see the Stage 2 report).

What each parameter does to the 1.7 s trot

With the period fixed at 1.7 s, five other settings were varied together: duty (the fraction of the cycle a foot is down), commanded speed, stance height, servo gain and swing height. That is 192 runs, 3 cycles each, at the placeholder 1.72 kg, one run per cell. Every point below is the median over the runs that walked, with the other four parameters spread over their values, so it describes this grid and not one parameter alone. The speed ratio is measured forward speed divided by commanded speed (1 would be the commanded speed). "Walked" only means upright and finished, not that the speed was met.

    Best settings in this grid

    For each commanded speed, the run that walked with the highest speed ratio. Three of the four winning values (30 mm swing, kp 30, 0.19 m stance) are the edge of the range tested, so the optimum may lie beyond them; the next section tests that. One run per cell, so these are the best of 48 single runs, not proven optima.

    What this does not show. Why each parameter has its effect (not traced); interactions between parameters; mass, friction, turning or terrain; any real robot. The speed shortfall looks like a roughly fixed amount rather than a fixed fraction (the ratio rises with commanded speed), but I did not fit or trace it. One run per cell, no randomisation, so there is no spread to quote.

    Pushing the best settings further: one parameter at a time, 10 runs per value

    The grid above had its best values at the edge of what was tried, so each of four parameters was swept further from the best baseline (1.7 s, duty 0.5, 0.15 m/s, 0.19 m stance, 30 mm swing, kp 30, kd 0.5): kp up to 150 (kd tied to kp as 0.5 × √(kp/30)), kd from 0.1 to 2, swing height down to 10 mm, stance height down to 0.09 m. The simulation is deterministic, so each value was run 10 times with a random draw of body masses (±5%) and servo latency (18 to 22 ms), the same 10 draws at every value. The mean and standard deviation below are over those 10 runs. The spread comes from those perturbations only: it is small because they are small, it says nothing about a real robot, and it leaves out model error.

    What this does not show. Each parameter was changed alone from one baseline, so the best values were not run together. Duty, commanded speed and mass were not varied. At stance heights of 0.15 m and below the commanded feet leave the joint limits (the run is not counted as walked, though the robot did not fall); the speed ratio there is not a valid test of the gait. At kd 2 the body moves backward, yet the walked flag (upright and finished) is still true. Why each effect occurs was not traced. Placeholder dimensions, masses, servo figures and friction.

    Do the best values add up? Together, at four speeds and three masses
